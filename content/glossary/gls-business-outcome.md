## Definition

**What the client wants to be true of their business, stated in their words.** A business
outcome is the top of the value chain and the start of the conversation — never a technology
goal.

The recognised outcomes — the names are the source's; the second column is the plain-language
concern each one points at, offered so a room can recognise its own problem in the list:

| Outcome | The concern it names |
|---|---|
| Working capital | Cash tied up in the balance sheet |
| Procurement and spend | What is bought, from whom, at what price |
| Inventory and supply reliability | Stock accuracy and the ability to supply |
| Revenue leakage | Money earned but not billed or collected |
| Process and compliance exposure | Controls, audit and regulatory risk |
| Digital transformation readiness | Fitness of the data for the target system |

*Reduce unplanned downtime* was a **candidate** outcome (R3, 2026-08-04), pending confirmation
that the chain walks all the way down to rules for it. Walkability validation on 2026-08-06 **cut
it** — 6 rules across 2 drivers — and it is held as a re-propose candidate once manufacturing
rules land. See [[con-outcome-hierarchy-l1-l5]] for the published six-outcome catalogue and the
inclusion criterion.

## Usage

The outcome is what the client picks first; the method then walks down through value lever to
key issue and on to the rules that measure it. Each outcome exports an [[gls-outcome-category]]
— one of the value library's six — which also renders as a sub-label on the outcome card.

> [!important]
> An outcome **never carries money of its own**. Currency aggregates at the value lever and
> only at the lever. An outcome is worth what its selected levers are worth.

## Say / never say

- **Say**: "reduce working capital tied up in slow-moving stock".
- **Never say**: "improve material master data quality" — that is a technology goal wearing an
  outcome's clothes, and it is the failure mode the whole method exists to avoid.
